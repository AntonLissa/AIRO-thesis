\chapter{Proof of Concept Evaluation}
\label{sec:poc evaluation}

\section{Evaluation Objectives}
\label{sec:evaluation objectives}

The objective of the Proof of Concept (PoC) evaluation is to assess the ability of FUNES to provide context-aware decision support in a representative Earth Observation ground segment scenario. The evaluation focuses on the core capabilities introduced by the proposed architecture, with particular emphasis on the retrieval and integration of heterogeneous operational information, the identification of anomalies and their operational impact, and the generation of appropriate decision-support outputs.

The evaluation is structured to progressively assess the capabilities required by FUNES to support mission operators. At a first level, the evaluation considers the ability of the system to retrieve relevant information from individual operational sources. At a second level, it assesses the ability to correlate information originating from different mission domains, such as network monitoring, incident management, and mission planning. Finally, more complex tasks are considered, requiring the system to combine information from multiple sources, identify the resulting operational risks, and provide recommendations consistent with the available evidence and with the role of a Decision Support System.

A further objective is to assess the contribution of the proposed retrieval and multi-agent orchestration approach with respect to a general-purpose Large Language Model operating directly on the complete operational context. For this purpose, the responses generated by FUNES are compared with those produced by a baseline LLM (llama 3.3 70b instruct) under the same operational scenario. The comparison is intended to evaluate whether the system architecture can improve the ability to identify relevant information, correlate operational evidence, and provide decision-support outputs while limiting the amount of contextual information that must be supplied to the underlying language model.

The evaluation therefore addresses the following main objectives:

\begin{itemize}
\item assess the capability of FUNES to retrieve relevant information from heterogeneous operational sources;
\item evaluate the ability to correlate information across different mission domains;
\item assess the identification of operational anomalies and their impact on planned satellite activities;
\item evaluate the accuracy of planning-related information and the identification of affected operations;
\item assess the ability of the system to filter irrelevant information and focus on the elements relevant to the operator's query;
\item evaluate whether the generated outputs are consistent with the role of a Decision Support System, supporting rather than replacing human decision-making;
\item assess the quality and operational consistency of the recommendations generated for complex scenarios;
\item compare the resulting decision-support performance with that of a general-purpose LLM provided with the complete operational context.
\end{itemize}

The evaluation is intended as a validation of the PoC capabilities rather than as a comprehensive validation of a production-ready operational system. Consequently, the results are interpreted in terms of the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed approach within the considered representative scenario, rather than as a general measure of system performance across all possible mission operational conditions.


\section{Evaluation Scenario}
\label{sec:evaluation scenario}

A representative Earth Observation ground segment scenario was defined to evaluate the Proof of Concept under operational conditions requiring the analysis and correlation of heterogeneous information sources. The scenario, depicted in Fig. \ref{fig:evaluation_scenario}, was designed to reproduce, at a simplified level, the type of information fragmentation that characterizes a real mission operations environment, where the assessment of an operational situation may require information from mission planning, network monitoring, incident management, and mission knowledge sources.

The scenario includes three ground stations, namely GS-01, GS-02, and GS-03, and four satellites, namely SAT-01, SAT-02, SAT-03, and SAT-04. SAT-01 is nominally served by GS-01, SAT-02 and SAT-03 by GS-02, and SAT-04 by GS-03. GS-01 is additionally connected to GS-02 and GS-03 through inter-station network links, introducing dependencies between the operational status of different ground stations.

The operational context was constructed from four main information domains: mission planning, network monitoring, incident and change management, and mission knowledge. The resulting scenario provides both nominal operational information and intentionally introduced anomalies, allowing the evaluation to consider not only direct information retrieval but also cross-domain correlation and operational impact analysis.

\subsection{Mission Planning}

For each satellite, approximately 36 hours of mission planning data were generated around the evaluation period. The planning information is structured as a sequence of satellite passages, with each passage associated with a ground station and a set of scheduled activities. Each activity contains its type, temporal interval, and the antenna on which it is scheduled.

The activities are not identical across passages and may include different operational types, such as uplink (UPL) and downlink (DWL) operations. This variability was introduced to provide a realistic planning context in which the impact of an unavailable resource cannot be determined solely from the satellite or ground station identifier, but requires inspection of the actual scheduled activities and their temporal characteristics.

The planning dataset therefore enables questions involving different levels of temporal and operational reasoning, including the retrieval of the next satellite pass, identification of scheduled activities, identification of affected acquisitions, and assessment of potential planning conflicts.

\subsection{Ground Segment and Network Status}

The scenario contains network monitoring information for all three ground stations and their associated antennas. For each station, the network data include the overall station status, backhaul capacity and utilization, average latency, and packet loss. At antenna level, the status, throughput, latency, packet loss, and utilization are provided.

The network scenario intentionally includes different operational conditions. GS-01 is in a degraded overall state, with antenna ANT-02 offline and ANT-03 exhibits increased latency and packet loss. The remaining antennas of GS-01 are operational. GS-02 and GS-03 are nominal at station level, although their individual resource utilization differs.

Inter-station connectivity is also included in the scenario. The connection between GS-01 and GS-02 is degraded, with reduced available bandwidth, increased latency, and packet loss. The connection between GS-01 and GS-03 is reported as operational in terms of connectivity status, but exhibits a significantly higher latency. These conditions introduce additional information that must be interpreted and filtered in relation to the actual operational activities and user questions rather than treated independently.

This configuration was deliberately designed to avoid a scenario in which the presence of an anomaly directly determines its operational consequence. For example, detecting that an antenna is unavailable is not sufficient to determine whether satellite operations are affected. The corresponding planning information must also be examined to identify the activities scheduled on the affected resource.

\subsection{Operational Events and Tickets}

The scenario includes a set of operational events representing anomalies, maintenance activities, and external operational requests. These events generate different types of operational records, including incident tickets, change tickets, and customer requests.

The main events include the unavailability of GS-01/ANT-02, degradation of the connectivity between GS-01 and GS-02, planned preventive maintenance on GS-02/ANT-02, and an additional customer acquisition request for the busy satellite SAT-01 of GS-01. These events have been associated with potential impacts on scheduled satellite activities, allowing the system to assess the relationship between an operational event and its consequences on mission planning.

To reproduce the presence of unrelated information typically encountered in operational environments, the scenario contains 32 tickets in total. Only six of these tickets are directly relevant to the operational events introduced in the scenario, while the remaining tickets describe plausible but unrelated operational issues and ground segments events. These additional records act as contextual noise and require the system to distinguish information relevant to the current operational situation from unrelated historical or concurrent events.

The inclusion of irrelevant but plausible tickets is particularly important for the evaluation of a decision-support system. In a realistic operational environment, the presence of a large amount of available information does not imply that all information is relevant to a given operator query. The ability to identify and filter the relevant evidence is therefore considered an integral part of the evaluation scenario.

\subsection{Mission Knowledge Base}

The scenario is complemented by a knowledge base containing 20 documents covering system informations and ECSS standards. These documents provide contextual knowledge that is not directly represented in the operational planning, network, or ticket data.

The knowledge base is used to support questions requiring background information about the mission and its systems, as well as questions in which operational observations need to be interpreted using previously defined system knowledge. Its inclusion allows the evaluation to distinguish between direct retrieval of operational data and the use of persistent domain knowledge during the reasoning process.

\subsection{Scenario Design Rationale}

The scenario was deliberately constructed to reproduce the main characteristics that motivate the FUNES architecture: heterogeneous information sources, temporal dependencies, operational anomalies, cross-domain relationships, and the presence of irrelevant information.

A scenario based exclusively on isolated retrieval questions would not adequately test the capabilities targeted by the PoC. For example, identifying that an antenna is offline is a relatively simple retrieval task. Determining whether this condition affects a satellite operation, however, requires the system to correlate the network status with the mission planning and to identify the activities assigned to the affected resource. Similarly, determining whether an operational issue requires immediate attention may require the combination of anomaly information, incident records, mission planning, and domain knowledge.

For this reason, the scenario was designed with increasing levels of information dependency. Some questions can be answered using a single information source, while others require the correlation of two or more sources. The most complex questions require the integration of multiple operational domains to identify current risks and formulate appropriate decision-support recommendations.

The presence of both relevant and irrelevant information was also intentional. The objective was not to create an artificially clean dataset in which every available record contributes to the answer, but to approximate the information overload encountered in operational environments. This allows the evaluation to consider not only whether FUNES can retrieve information, but also whether it can identify the information that is relevant to the specific operational question.

Overall, the scenario was designed to provide a controlled but sufficiently heterogeneous environment in which the principal capabilities of the proposed FUNES architecture can be exercised: retrieval of mission knowledge and operational data, correlation of information across different sources, identification of operational impacts, and generation of decision-support outputs.


\section{Evaluation Tasks and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:evaluation tasks and setup}

The evaluation was designed around a set of operational questions derived from a representative ground segment scenario. The questions were defined to progressively increase in complexity and to exercise the main capabilities provided by the FUNES architecture. In particular, three levels of task complexity were considered: single-source retrieval, multi-source correlation, and decision-support tasks.

\subsection{Evaluation Tasks}
\label{subsec: eval_task}

The first category consists of \textit{easy tasks}, which require the retrieval of information from a single operational source. These tasks are intended to verify the basic ability of the system to identify the appropriate information source and retrieve the relevant information. The considered sources include the knowledge base, mission planning, network monitoring, and incident management systems. Representative questions include queries regarding the architecture and operational interfaces of the Flight Operations System, the current network status, the status of a ground-station antenna, currently open incidents, and the next scheduled satellite passes.

The second category consists of \textit{medium tasks}, which require the retrieval and correlation of information from multiple operational sources. These tasks are designed to evaluate whether the system can combine heterogeneous information to derive operationally relevant conclusions. For example, determining why a ground station is unavailable requires the correlation of network information with incident records, while identifying the satellite acquisitions affected by an incident requires information from both the incident management and mission planning domains. Other tasks require the system to determine whether a ground station is available for a planned acquisition or whether an acquisition can be rescheduled based on the available planning information.

The third category consists of \textit{hard tasks}, which extend the previous category by requiring situational assessment and decision support. These tasks require the system to integrate information from multiple operational sources, identify relevant anomalies, determine their impact on mission activities, and, where appropriate, propose possible operational actions. Examples include identifying the current operational issues affecting the ground segment, determining which issues require immediate attention, identifying risks to planned activities for a given operational day, and summarizing current anomalies together with their operational impact and possible mitigation actions.

This progressive organization allows the evaluation to distinguish between the ability to retrieve individual pieces of information and the higher-level capabilities required for operational decision support. The complete set of evaluation questions is reported in Table \ref{tab:evaluation_questions}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The evaluation was conducted by comparing the responses generated by the FUNES PoC with those produced by a general-purpose Large Language Model used as a baseline. FUNES was evaluated using its complete processing pipeline, including the selection of the appropriate information sources, retrieval of the relevant operational context, correlation of the retrieved information, and generation of the final response.

As a baseline, \textit{Llama 3.3 70B Instruct} was provided with the complete operational context associated with the evaluation scenario. In contrast to FUNES, the baseline did not perform explicit source selection or selective retrieval. Instead, the entire available context, of around 24000 tokens, was provided directly to the model together with the user query. This configuration was selected to provide a reference for assessing the contribution of the retrieval and orchestration mechanisms implemented by FUNES. The model has the same system prompt and user prompt of the synthesizer agent of FUNES and the same  \texttt{temperature = 0}.

The two systems were evaluated using the same set of operational questions and the same underlying scenario. For each question, the generated response and, for FUNES, the information sources selected during the reasoning process were recorded. This allowed the evaluation to consider both the quality of the final answers and the behaviour of the underlying information-selection mechanism.

The resulting evaluation therefore considers two complementary aspects of the proposed architecture. First, the quality of the decision-support responses is assessed across tasks with increasing levels of complexity. Second, the behaviour of the information retrieval and orchestration mechanisms is analysed by considering tool selection and contextual input requirements. This setup provides a basis for evaluating not only whether the system produces appropriate answers, but also whether the architectural mechanisms introduced by FUNES contribute to the observed behaviour.


\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation methodology}

The evaluation was conducted through a scenario-based methodology designed to reproduce a representative operational situation within an Earth Observation ground segment described in \ref{subsec: eval_task}.
The evaluation was performed using two systems. The first system was the FUNES PoC, including its retrieval, tool-selection, multi-agent orchestration, and reasoning capabilities. The second system was a baseline based on the Llama 3.3 70B Instruct model. The baseline was provided with the complete operational context of around 24K tokens and was asked to answer the same questions without the selective retrieval and multi-source orchestration mechanisms implemented by FUNES. The final user prompt given to the baseline is the same that would be given to FUNES if every tool was called and every tool returned the full possible set of informations. This configuration was selected to provide a comparison between the proposed architecture and a general-purpose LLM operating directly on the available operational context.

For each question, both systems generated an answer independently. The resulting answers were subsequently evaluated using an LLM-as-a-Judge approach \cite{zheng2023judging}. Llama 3.3 70b instruct with \texttt{temperature = 0} was employed as an independent evaluator and was provided with the operational context, the question, and the two generated answers. The identities of the systems were not considered part of the evaluation criteria, and the assessment focused exclusively on the content and operational validity of the generated responses. For each question, the individual criterion scores were recorded and subsequently aggregated to enable comparison between FUNES and the baseline across the different task complexity levels and evaluation dimensions.

The use of an LLM-as-a-Judge was selected to provide a consistent evaluation procedure for responses where correctness cannot always be expressed through exact matching. In particular, complex decision-support questions may admit multiple valid formulations or operationally reasonable recommendations. The evaluator therefore assesses the consistency of each response with the evidence and operational constraints defined by the scenario rather than requiring an exact reference answer. To reduce potential evaluator bias related to the identity or position of the compared systems, the association between the two responses and the system identities was randomized during the evaluation.



The evaluation criteria were defined to reflect the main capabilities expected from an operational Decision Support System. Each criterion was evaluated using a three-level ordinal scale, where:

\begin{itemize}
   \item Score 0 - Incorrect: A score of 0 was assigned when the answer contained a material factual or operational error that directly affected the criterion being evaluated. This included, for example, false statements, contradictions with the operational context, incorrect correlations, materially inaccurate planning information, unsupported causal claims that affected the validity of the answer, or operational recommendations that were incompatible with the available evidence. Minor imprecisions or isolated omissions were not, by themselves, considered sufficient to warrant a score of 0.
   \item Score 1 - Partially correct: A score of 1 was assigned when the answer was substantially correct but exhibited one or more significant limitations preventing it from fully satisfying the criterion. Such limitations included the omission of relevant information, the establishment of only part of a required correlation, failure to identify an important operational impact, a limited error that did not invalidate the overall answer, or a recommendation that was relevant but overly generic or incomplete. Thus, a score of 1 indicated that the answer was generally useful and substantively correct, but did not completely meet the requirements of the criterion.
   \item Score 2 - Fully correct: A score of 2 was assigned when the answer fully satisfied the criterion for the specific question. To receive this score, the answer was required to provide the information requested, correctly incorporate the relevant operational evidence, establish supported correlations, accurately report planning information when required, and provide appropriate and evidence-consistent recommendations when requested.
\end{itemize}




The criteria were evaluated independently for each answer against the operational context provided to the evaluator. Non-applicable criteria were excluded from the total score rather than being assigned a zero value.

The following criteria were considered in the evaluation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Answer Correctness (AC):} evaluates whether the answer correctly addresses the question using the available operational evidence. This includes the correct reporting of requested information, values, identifiers, operational states, and conclusions, while penalizing factual errors and unsupported claims that materially affect the answer.

    \item \textbf{Anomaly Identification (AI):} evaluates the ability to identify and correctly characterize the operational anomalies relevant to the question. The criterion considers whether relevant anomalies, their status, and associated technical information are correctly reported without introducing unrelated or unsupported anomalies.

    \item \textbf{Evidence Correlation (EC):} evaluates the ability to establish correct relationships between evidence originating from different operational sources, such as network monitoring, incident reports, planning information, and the knowledge base. Unsupported causal or operational relationships are not considered valid correlations.

    \item \textbf{Noise Filtering (NF):} evaluates the ability to distinguish information relevant to the question from unrelated information contained in the operational context. A high score indicates that the answer uses relevant evidence without introducing unrelated information or unsupported relationships.

    \item \textbf{DSS Role Adherence (DSS):} evaluates whether the answer behaves consistently with the role of an operational Decision Support System. The criterion considers whether the system provides evidence-based situational awareness, appropriately identifies operational implications and uncertainties, supports human decision-making, and avoids presenting unsupported conclusions or assuming autonomous operational authority.

    \item \textbf{Operational Recommendation Quality (ORQ):} evaluates the quality of operational recommendations when the question explicitly requires mitigation actions or a proposed course of action. Recommendations are assessed according to their relevance, consistency with the available evidence, operational usefulness, and respect for known constraints and uncertainties.
\end{itemize}

Not all evaluation criteria are applicable to every question. The applicable criteria depend on the information and reasoning capabilities required by the specific task. For example, a single-source network status query primarily evaluates information retrieval and anomaly identification, whereas a question concerning the impact of an incident on scheduled acquisitions requires evidence correlation and planning accuracy. Similarly, recommendation-oriented questions require the additional assessment of Decision Support System adherence and operational action quality. This criterion-dependent evaluation avoids penalising a response for not providing information or reasoning that was not required by the question, Table \ref{tab:criteria_applicability} shows what criteria are applicable to each question.


The methodology is intended to provide a comparative evaluation of the PoC rather than a statistically exhaustive validation of an operational system. The results should therefore be interpreted within the scope of the considered scenario, question set, and evaluation procedure. In particular, the use of an external LLM as an evaluator introduces an additional source of potential evaluation bias, while the proprietary nature of the evaluator prevents complete inspection of its internal decision process. These aspects are considered when discussing the results and limitations of the evaluation.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:evaluation discussion}


\subsection{Overall Comparison}

The overall results indicate that FUNES provides a substantial improvement in the evaluation score of the generated responses compared with the baseline within the considered evaluation scenario. FUNES achieves an overall score of 89.9\%, compared with 63.0\% obtained by Llama 3.3 70B Instruct, corresponding to a difference of 26.9 percentage points. The performance advantage is consistently observed across all three task-complexity categories, with FUNES obtaining higher aggregate scores for easy, medium, and hard tasks.

The observed improvement is particularly relevant considering the configuration of the baseline. Llama 3.3 70B Instruct was provided with the complete operational context of approximately 24K tokens, including the information available across the different operational sources considered in the scenario. Therefore, the performance difference cannot be attributed simply to the baseline lacking access to the information required to answer the questions. Rather, the results indicate that providing the complete context to a general-purpose LLM does not necessarily result in the most effective use of the available operational information. In contrast, FUNES explicitly selects and retrieves information according to the user query before generating the final response, this allows the system to focus on the most relevant information for each specific query and avoid to get lost in irrelevant details \cite{liu-etal-2024-lost}. The observed improvement is consistent with the hypothesis that selective information retrieval and filtering contribute to the higher performance of FUNES, particularly for easy tasks where the questions are highly specific and the relevant information can be retrieved from a single source.

This result is consistent with one of the main objectives of the proposed architecture, namely to support the underlying language model by controlling how heterogeneous operational information is selected and integrated into the reasoning process. The improvement observed at the aggregate level suggests that the retrieval and orchestration mechanisms implemented by FUNES can contribute to more reliable decision-support responses within the considered scenario. However, the aggregate comparison alone does not establish that each individual architectural component is responsible for the observed improvement, as no ablation study was performed. The detailed results are therefore analysed in the following sections to identify the task types and evaluation criteria for which FUNES provides the largest benefits, as well as the cases in which the baseline achieves comparable or better performance.



\subsection{Effect of task complexity}

\subsubsection{Easy task}

The results for easy tasks show the largest performance difference between FUNES and the baseline. As reported in Table \ref{tab:performance_difficulty}, FUNES achieves 48/50 points (96.0\%), compared with 23/50 points (46.0\%) obtained by the baseline. The difference is particularly pronounced for questions requiring the retrieval of information from a single source, where the task complexity is primarily associated with identifying the relevant information rather than performing complex operational reasoning.

A qualitative analysis of the individual questions provides an indication of the type of behaviour underlying this difference. For questions Q2 and Q3, which require the retrieval of document-based mission knowledge, the baseline included additional information concerning the current ground-segment operational situation, despite this information not being required to answer the questions. The responses therefore combined the requested document-based information with unrelated operational context. In contrast, FUNES selected the Knowledge Base as the relevant information source and restricted the retrieved context to the information required for answering the query. This resulted in higher scores for both answer correctness and noise filtering.

A more pronounced example is provided by Q5, which asks for the number of passages scheduled for SAT-03 on a specific date. The baseline produced an incorrect count of 32 passages and additionally included information about the network status, operational tickets, potential impacts, and recommended actions. The relevant planning information was therefore not only incorrectly interpreted, but was also accompanied by substantial unrelated context. FUNES, instead, retrieved the relevant planning information and identified the 16 passages corresponding to PASS-017 through PASS-032, providing a concise answer focused on the requested information. The resulting score was 4/4 for FUNES compared with 0/4 for the baseline across the applicable evaluation criteria.

These examples suggest that, for simple retrieval-oriented tasks, the availability of the complete operational context does not necessarily lead to effective use of the relevant information. The baseline had access to the information required by the query, but its response included unrelated operational information and, in Q5, produced an incorrect result. FUNES instead used its source-selection and retrieval mechanisms to constrain the context available to the subsequent reasoning process. The observed behaviour is therefore consistent with the intended role of the FUNES architecture in reducing irrelevant contextual information before answer generation. However, these examples alone do not establish a causal relationship between the individual architectural components and the observed performance improvement, as the evaluation does not include an ablation study.



\subsubsection{Medium Tasks}

The results for medium tasks show a smaller but still consistent performance advantage for FUNES compared with the baseline. FUNES achieves 40/46 points (87.0\%), while Llama 3.3 70B Instruct achieves 33/46 points (71.7\%). Compared with the easy tasks, the difference between the two systems is therefore substantially reduced. This result suggests that the advantage associated with selective information retrieval becomes less pronounced when the task requires a higher degree of cross-source reasoning and operational interpretation. At the same time, FUNES maintains a higher aggregate performance, indicating that the proposed architecture continues to provide benefits for tasks involving multiple information sources.

A representative example is Q11, which asks why GS-01 is offline and requires the correlation of network monitoring information with incident and problem management records. FUNES obtained the maximum score of 8/8, while the baseline obtained 3/8. The baseline correctly identified the connectivity problem affecting GS-01/ANT-02, but extended the explanation to the degraded status of GS-01/ANT-03 without sufficient evidence that this condition was responsible for the station-level status. It also included information about SAT-01 scheduled activities and operational recommendations that were not required to answer the question. FUNES, instead, correlated the relevant network and ticket information while maintaining a more focused response. This example illustrates the potential benefit of explicit source selection and information correlation when the answer depends on evidence distributed across multiple operational domains.

However, the results also show that the use of multi-source orchestration does not guarantee an improvement for every medium-complexity task. This is particularly evident in Q16, concerning the urgent acquisition request for SAT-01. In this case, the baseline achieved the maximum score of 12/12, whereas FUNES obtained 8/12. Both systems correctly identified the degraded condition of GS-01 and the relevant antenna anomalies, but the baseline provided a more direct assessment of the specific acquisition request and proposed alternative options for addressing it. FUNES instead focused primarily on the general resolution of the connectivity and antenna issues and did not sufficiently address the specific feasibility of the urgent acquisition request. The judge therefore assigned lower scores to FUNES for answer correctness, evidence correlation, noise filtering, and operational recommendation quality.

The contrast between Q11 and Q16 is relevant for interpreting the role of the FUNES architecture. In Q11, the main challenge is to identify and correlate evidence distributed across different sources, which corresponds closely to the capabilities targeted by the retrieval and orchestration mechanisms. In Q16, however, the retrieved information must subsequently be transformed into a specific operational assessment and recommendation. The results therefore suggest that effective information retrieval and correlation are important but not sufficient by themselves to guarantee high-quality decision support. The final reasoning and synthesis stage remains critical, particularly when the question requires the system to evaluate the feasibility of a specific operational action rather than simply establish the relationship between available evidence.

Overall, the medium-task results indicate that FUNES retains an advantage when compared with a general-purpose LLM operating on the complete operational context, but that this advantage is less pronounced than for single-source retrieval tasks. The individual results also show that the benefit of the proposed architecture is task-dependent: explicit retrieval and orchestration appear particularly useful when relevant evidence must be identified and correlated across heterogeneous sources, while tasks requiring a more specific operational judgement may still be handled more effectively by the baseline. These observations provide a basis for analysing the more complex decision-support tasks considered in the following subsection.



\subsubsection{Hard Task}
The results for hard tasks show a smaller performance difference between FUNES and the baseline than that observed for easy tasks. FUNES achieves 36/42 points (85.7\%), while the baseline achieves 31/42 points (73.8\%). The reduced performance gap is particularly relevant for these tasks, as they require the integration of heterogeneous information, the identification of operational consequences, and, in several cases, the formulation of operational recommendations. In contrast to the easy tasks, where the main challenge is often the identification of the relevant information, the hard tasks place a greater emphasis on the subsequent interpretation and integration of the retrieved evidence.

The results also show that the advantage of FUNES is not uniform across complex decision-support tasks. A representative counterexample is Q19, which asks the systems to identify risks affecting activities planned for a specific day and to report the corresponding affected activities and datetimes. In this case, the baseline achieved the maximum score of 10/10, while FUNES obtained 7/10. The judge particularly penalized FUNES for failing to provide the specific affected activities and corresponding datetimes requested by the question. Although FUNES correctly identified the relevant anomalies and their general operational implications, it did not complete the reasoning chain required by the query, namely linking these anomalies to the specific affected activities and their corresponding datetimes. This indicates that retrieving the relevant operational information does not by itself guarantee that the final synthesis will fully exploit the available evidence.

Q20 provides a contrasting example and represents the most comprehensive task in the benchmark, requiring the identification of current anomalies, the assessment of their operational impact, and the formulation of possible operational actions. FUNES achieved the maximum score of 12/12, while the baseline obtained 8/12. The judge identified differences in anomaly identification, evidence correlation, and recommendation quality as the main sources of the performance gap. In particular, the FUNES response successfully incorporated a wider set of relevant anomalies, including the degraded resources at GS-01 and the network latency issue affecting GS-03, and connected these observations to their potential operational consequences. The resulting recommendations were also considered more specific and consistent with the available evidence.

The contrasting results of Q19 and Q20 are particularly relevant to the interpretation of the overall hard-task performance. They show that FUNES can obtain high scores on complex operational reasoning when the relevant information is correctly retrieved and integrated, but that the orchestration and retrieval process does not eliminate the possibility of errors during the final synthesis. Conversely, the baseline can occasionally exploit its access to the complete operational context to construct a more complete answer, as observed in Q19. Therefore, the results do not support the interpretation that selective retrieval is inherently superior to providing the complete context. Rather, they suggest that the benefit of selective retrieval depends on the ability of the architecture to preserve the relationships between the retrieved information and the specific requirements of the query.

This behaviour also provides a possible explanation for the smaller performance gap observed for hard tasks. As task complexity increases, the contribution of simple information filtering becomes less dominant, while the quality of cross-source reasoning and final answer synthesis becomes increasingly important. Both systems rely on the underlying language model for these capabilities, which may reduce the relative advantage provided by FUNES's retrieval and orchestration mechanisms. At the same time, the limited number of hard questions and applicable criterion evaluations prevents drawing strong conclusions about a systematic relationship between task complexity and relative performance. The observed results should therefore be interpreted as evidence of different system behaviours within the considered benchmark rather than as a general performance law.

\subsection{Where FUNES Helps and Where It Does Not}
\label{sec:where funes helps}

The individual task results provide a more detailed view of the conditions under which FUNES provides the largest benefits. Overall, the observed improvements are particularly evident in tasks where the main challenge is to identify the appropriate information source, retrieve the relevant evidence, and restrict the reasoning process to the information required by the query. This behaviour is especially evident in retrieval-oriented questions, but also extends to tasks requiring the correlation of information from multiple operational sources. In contrast, the advantage becomes less consistent when the task requires a highly specific operational assessment or when the final answer depends on preserving detailed relationships between anomalies, planned activities, and their corresponding operational consequences.

For document-based retrieval tasks, FUNES appears particularly effective at limiting irrelevant contextual information. The results for Q2, Q3, and Q5 illustrate this behaviour. In these cases, the baseline had access to the same underlying information but tended to incorporate additional operational information that was not required by the question. FUNES instead selected the appropriate knowledge source and generated responses based on a more focused context. The improvement observed in these cases is therefore not primarily related to access to additional information, but to the ability to select and constrain the information provided to the language model. This distinction is relevant in operational environments, where a large amount of simultaneously available information may increase the risk of producing responses that are technically informed but poorly aligned with the specific user request.

A similar benefit can be observed for tasks requiring the correlation of heterogeneous evidence. Q11 provides a representative example, where the answer requires combining information from network monitoring and incident or problem management sources. FUNES achieved the maximum score in this task, suggesting that explicit retrieval and orchestration can be beneficial when the relevant evidence is distributed across different operational domains. In such cases, the main contribution of the architecture is not simply retrieving individual facts, but determining which sources should contribute to the answer and combining their information before final response generation.

However, the results also identify clear limitations. FUNES does not consistently outperform the baseline when the task requires a specific operational judgement or a detailed mapping between evidence and an intended action. In Q16, for example, the baseline obtained a higher score because it more directly addressed the feasibility of the urgent acquisition request and considered possible alternatives, whereas FUNES focused more strongly on the underlying infrastructure anomalies. Similarly, in Q19, FUNES correctly identified relevant anomalies but did not fully connect them to the specific affected activities and datetimes requested by the user. These cases suggest that successful retrieval and evidence correlation are not sufficient to guarantee an adequate decision-support response. The system must also preserve the task-specific objective during the final synthesis stage and translate the retrieved evidence into the exact operational information requested.

Q20 provides a complementary example in which FUNES successfully performs this final synthesis. The task requires anomaly identification, assessment of operational impact, and formulation of possible actions. FUNES achieved the maximum score and outperformed the baseline across all applicable criteria. This result indicates that the limitations observed in Q16 and Q19 should not be interpreted as an inherent inability of FUNES to perform decision-support tasks. Rather, performance depends on whether the retrieved evidence is correctly aligned with the specific requirements of the query and subsequently transformed into an appropriately scoped operational response.

Taken together, these observations suggest that the main strength of FUNES lies in controlling the information flow preceding the final reasoning stage. The architecture is particularly effective when information overload, source heterogeneity, or irrelevant context represents a significant part of the challenge. Its benefits become less predictable when the dominant difficulty shifts from information selection to detailed operational reasoning and answer synthesis. This distinction is important because it indicates that FUNES should not be viewed as a replacement for the reasoning capabilities of the underlying language model, but rather as an architectural mechanism for providing that model with a more appropriate and task-relevant information context.

These findings should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. The analysis is based on a limited number of representative questions and does not include an ablation study capable of isolating the contribution of individual FUNES components. The observed patterns therefore describe the behaviour of the evaluated system in the considered benchmark rather than establishing that a specific architectural mechanism is solely responsible for a given improvement or failure.


\subsection{Architectural Implications}
\label{sec:architectural implications}

The evaluation results provide several indications regarding the effectiveness of the architectural choices adopted in FUNES. Although the evaluation does not include an ablation study and therefore cannot isolate the contribution of individual components, the observed behaviour is consistent with the design objectives of the proposed architecture. In particular, the results highlight the importance of controlled information acquisition, agent specialization, and iterative evidence refinement in operational decision-support scenarios.

One of the most relevant implications concerns the separation between information acquisition and response generation. In the baseline configuration, the LLM receives the complete operational context and is responsible for determining which information is relevant to the query, this configuration is comparable to a setting in which the Synthesizer Agent receives the complete output of all available sources without prior filtering. In the evaluated tasks, this configuration was associated with the inclusion of irrelevant information and, in some cases, incorrect conclusions. FUNES instead introduces an explicit planning stage through the Planner Agent, which determines which information sources should be queried before the final response is generated. The strong performance observed for retrieval-oriented tasks is therefore consistent with the architectural decision to control the information flow before the reasoning stage rather than providing the complete operational context to a single LLM.

The results are also compatible with the intended role of specialized agents and tools for heterogeneous operational sources. The Knowledge Base, Planning, Network, and Ticket tools expose different types of information through a common interface, while specialized agents are responsible for adapting queries and filtering source-specific information. This separation is particularly relevant for medium-complexity tasks, where the information required to answer a question may be distributed across multiple operational domains. The performance observed in Q11, for example, is consistent with the intended role of the Planner and specialized retrieval components, since the answer requires the correlation of information originating from different sources. Rather than requiring a single LLM to interpret the complete operational context, the architecture decomposes information acquisition into source-specific operations and subsequently provides their results to the Synthesizer Agent.


At the same time, the results suggest that the current critic-driven refinement mechanism could be extended beyond missing information retrieval. In Q19, the main limitation was not necessarily the absence of the relevant operational information, but the incomplete synthesis of the available evidence with respect to the exact requirements of the query. A refinement mechanism that evaluates not only whether sufficient evidence has been retrieved, but also whether each requested element has been explicitly addressed, could therefore improve the robustness of the final response. For example, the Critic could verify that a question asking for affected activities and datetimes results in an explicit mapping between each identified anomaly and the corresponding planned activity. This would extend the role of the Critic from evidence sufficiency assessment towards requirement-level answer validation.

The results also highlight the importance of the Synthesizer Agent as a distinct architectural component. The comparison between Q16, Q19, and Q20 suggests that the main challenge in complex tasks is not always information retrieval. In Q16 and Q19, the available evidence was not fully reflected in the final response with respect to the specific requirements of the query. Conversely, in Q20, the system successfully integrated anomalies, operational impacts, and recommendations and obtained the maximum score. This variability indicates that the final synthesis stage remains a critical component of the architecture. Consequently, future improvements should not focus exclusively on retrieval quality, but should also strengthen the ability of the Synthesizer Agent to preserve the structure and intent of the original query during evidence synthesis.

The architecture therefore suggests a division of responsibilities between information selection and operational reasoning. The Planner and specialized agents primarily control which information enters the reasoning process, while the Synthesizer Agent is responsible for transforming that information into an operational assessment. The evaluation indicates that this separation is beneficial, particularly when irrelevant context is a major source of error, but that the boundary between retrieval and reasoning must remain sufficiently flexible for complex decision-support tasks. In particular, aggressive information filtering could potentially remove contextual relationships that are necessary for a complete operational assessment. The objective should therefore not be to minimize the amount of retrieved information, but to maximize its relevance to the specific requirements of the query.

Another architectural implication concerns the use of a shared state and graph-based orchestration. The evaluation tasks increasingly require information obtained at different stages of the investigation to be combined into a single response. The shared \texttt{AgentState} provides a persistent representation of the query, execution plan, retrieved evidence, synthesized response, and critic feedback, allowing subsequent investigation cycles to build upon previous results. This mechanism is particularly suitable for the iterative nature of operational investigations, where an initially observed anomaly may require additional queries to determine its cause and operational impact.
The Q20 task illustrates a use case for which maintaining evidence collected from multiple operational sources is important. The shared state and graph-based orchestration mechanisms described in Section \ref{sec: state management} are designed to support this type of iterative evidence integration

Finally, the results suggest that the main architectural advantage of FUNES should not be interpreted as the replacement of the underlying LLM reasoning capability. Instead, the architecture acts as a control layer around the LLM, determining which information is retrieved, how heterogeneous evidence is progressively collected, and when the generated assessment should be reconsidered. This distinction is important when interpreting the comparison with Llama 3.3 70B Instruct. The baseline benefits from a larger undifferentiated context, whereas FUNES introduces explicit mechanisms for information selection, source specialization, orchestration, and iterative validation. The observed 26.9 percentage-point improvement therefore provides empirical support for the overall architectural strategy, but does not establish that any single component is responsible for the improvement.




\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:evaluation limitations}

The results presented in this evaluation should be interpreted in light of several limitations concerning the benchmark design, the comparison methodology, and the evaluation procedure.

First, the evaluation is based on a limited number of questions within a single controlled operational scenario. Although the questions cover different task-complexity levels and involve heterogeneous operational information sources, they represent only a subset of the situations that may occur in a real satellite ground-segment environment. In particular, the benchmark includes 20 questions in total, with 10 easy, 6 medium, and 4 hard questions. The relatively small number of questions, especially for the medium- and hard-complexity categories, limits the statistical robustness of comparisons within individual task-complexity levels. In particular, the results for hard tasks are based on only four questions and may therefore be sensitive to the specific characteristics of the selected test cases. The observed performance differences should therefore be interpreted as evidence of the behaviour of the two evaluated systems within the considered benchmark, rather than as evidence of general performance across operational scenarios.

Second, the comparison considers a single baseline model, Llama 3.3 70B Instruct. The results therefore establish a comparison between FUNES and this specific baseline configuration, but do not determine whether similar improvements would be obtained with other LLMs or alternative prompting and context-management strategies. In particular, the observed performance difference should not be interpreted as evidence that FUNES universally outperforms general-purpose LLMs.

Third, the experiments were conducted through the Groq API, which imposes limits on the number of requests and the number of tokens that can be processed within a given time period. These constraints limited the extent to which the evaluation could be repeated under identical experimental conditions and may therefore affect the reproducibility of large-scale repeated evaluations. The benchmark was consequently evaluated under the available API constraints rather than through an unrestricted experimental setup. Future evaluations could mitigate this limitation by using an inference environment with fewer restrictions, allowing a larger number of repeated runs and a more robust estimation of performance variability.

Fourth, the evaluation does not include an ablation study of the FUNES architecture. The system combines several mechanisms, including source selection, specialized retrieval components, orchestration, iterative evidence refinement, and final response synthesis. Since these components are evaluated as part of the complete system, the benchmark cannot isolate the contribution of any individual architectural mechanism to the observed performance. The results therefore support conclusions about the behaviour of FUNES as a complete architecture, but not causal conclusions about the effectiveness of individual components.

Fifth, the evaluation relies on an LLM-based judge to assign scores according to the defined evaluation criteria. Although the criteria and scoring scale were explicitly specified and the judge was instructed to evaluate the answers against the operational context, LLM-based evaluation may still introduce evaluator-dependent variability. The obtained scores should therefore be interpreted as the outcome of the adopted evaluation procedure rather than as an absolute ground truth of response quality. A complementary evaluation involving human domain experts, multiple independent judges, or inter-rater agreement analysis would provide stronger evidence for the reliability of the evaluation.

Sixth, the benchmark uses a controlled operational context rather than live operational data. The evaluation therefore does not assess the behaviour of FUNES under conditions such as continuously changing system states, concurrent operational events, incomplete or inconsistent information, real-time timing constraints, or unexpected failures. These factors may affect both retrieval and decision-support performance in an operational deployment and should be considered in future validation activities.

Finally, the aggregate scoring procedure assigns the same maximum weight to each applicable evaluation criterion. This provides a simple and consistent comparison between the two systems, but it does not necessarily reflect the relative operational importance of different error types. For example, an incorrect operational recommendation may have substantially different consequences from the inclusion of a limited amount of irrelevant information. A future evaluation could therefore incorporate criterion-specific weighting or risk-oriented metrics reflecting the operational criticality of different types of errors.

These limitations constrain the interpretation and generalizability of the observed differences between FUNES and the baseline. The results provide evidence of the relative behaviour of the two complete systems within the considered benchmark, but broader claims regarding generalization across task types and operational scenarios, component-level effectiveness, evaluation robustness, and real-world deployment require further validation.
